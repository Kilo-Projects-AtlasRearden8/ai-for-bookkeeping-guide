# 1. AI Bookkeeping Basics

Bookkeeping is just the habit of writing down what your business earns and what
it spends. AI can help you do it faster, but it does not replace the habit
itself. Think of AI as a careful assistant who can sort a messy pile of
receipts, draft categories, and remind you of deadlines — but you are still the
bookkeeper in charge.

## What AI can help with

- Turning a list of messy bank descriptions into clean categories
- Drafting income and expense records from notes or receipts
- Answering questions like "What counts as a home office expense?"
- Building spreadsheets and checklists you can print
- Reminding you of quarterly tax deadlines

## What AI should not do

- File your tax return for you
- Decide what you can deduct without you checking
- Handle your bank logins or move money
- Give you final answers about your specific tax situation

## Pick your tools

You do not need expensive software to start. A spreadsheet (Google Sheets,
Excel, or LibreOffice Calc) plus a notebook works fine. AI tools can be:

- A general AI assistant you chat with
- A spreadsheet with AI features built in
- Receipt-scanning apps that read text from photos

Choose tools that let you export your data (CSV or Excel), so you are never
locked into one app.

## Privacy checklist for financial AI

Do this before you paste anything into an AI tool:

- [ ] Remove real bank account numbers and routing numbers
- [ ] Remove Social Security numbers and taxpayer ID numbers
- [ ] Remove customer names, addresses, and credit card numbers
- [ ] Use generic descriptions ("Client A," "Office supply store")
- [ ] Keep your master spreadsheet on your own computer or drive
- [ ] Read the AI tool's privacy policy: does it train on your data?

If you are unsure, keep the data offline and type only fake examples into the AI.

## Simple workflow you can repeat every week

1. **Gather** — Pull your bank and payment-app transactions for the week.
2. **Record** — Enter income and expenses in your spreadsheet.
3. **Sort** — Use AI to suggest categories for anything unclear.
4. **File** — Save receipts (paper or scan) by month.
5. **Review** — Spend 15 minutes checking that everything looks right.

Doing a little every week keeps quarterly tax prep from becoming a crisis.

## Copy-and-paste prompts

Prompt 1 — Clean up a transaction list:

```
I am keeping my own small-business bookkeeping records. Below is a list of
bank transactions with messy descriptions. For each one, suggest:
1) a clean plain-English description, and
2) an expense category (use common Schedule C categories like Advertising,
Office expenses, Supplies, Rent, Utilities, Meals, Travel, etc.).

Mark anything you are unsure about with [?]. Do not give tax advice.

Paste transactions here:
...
```

Prompt 2 — Ask whether something is likely deductible:

```
I run a [type of business, e.g. freelance writing / reselling / consulting].
I spent $[amount] on [describe item]. Based on general IRS Publication 535
guidance for ordinary and necessary business expenses, what questions should I
ask my tax professional before I deduct this? Do not tell me whether to deduct
it.

Education only — I will verify with a CPA or enrolled agent.
```

Prompt 3 — Build a monthly summary:

```
I will paste my income and expense totals for the month. Create a simple
summary table with total income, total expenses, and net profit. Then list the
top three expense categories by dollar amount. Keep it in plain English.

Education only — not tax advice.
...
```

## Key terms in plain English

- **Income**: Money your business receives.
- **Expense**: Money your business spends to operate.
- **Category**: A label for a type of spending (for example, "Office supplies").
- **Deduction**: An expense the tax rules let you subtract from income before
  calculating tax.
- **Net profit**: Income minus expenses.
- **Estimated tax**: Tax you pay to the IRS four times a year instead of all at
  once (Chapter 5).
