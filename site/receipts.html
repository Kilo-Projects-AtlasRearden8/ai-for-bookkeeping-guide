4. Receipts

A receipt is proof that you spent money. If the IRS ever asks about a deduction, your bank statement alone may not be enough — you usually need the receipt or invoice too.

What to keep

Keep receipts and invoices for:

  • Anything you plan to deduct as a business expense
  • Equipment and supplies
  • Meals and travel you claim for business
  • Home office purchases
  • Anything over a small amount (your tax pro can suggest a threshold)

You generally do not need to keep receipts for tiny everyday cash purchases that you do not claim, but when in doubt, keep it.

How to store receipts

Choose one system and use it all year:

  • Paper — Folders or envelopes labeled by month. Simple and works during power outages.
  • Photos/scans — Take a photo of each receipt right away, before it fades. Name files like 2026-03-14_Staples_office-supplies.jpg so you can find them later.
  • Digital — If you receive email receipts, save them to a folder (do not leave them only in your inbox).

Back up digital receipts to your own computer or drive. Do not keep your only copy in an app that could shut down or change its terms.

Retention: how long to keep them

A common rule of thumb is to keep tax records for at least three years from the date you file your return, and longer if you claim certain things (for example, assets you depreciate). Ask your tax professional how long to keep records for your situation, and check IRS.gov.

AI and receipts

AI can read a photo or PDF of a receipt and turn it into a line for your spreadsheet: date, store, total, category. Use it as a first draft, then check the numbers against the image.
